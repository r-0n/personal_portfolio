Concept & Inspiration

Rethinking the Driving Interface

Exploring embodied interaction through a miniature driving experience.

Learning to drive involves coordinating several actions at once. Steering, accelerating, and braking must work together, often requiring practice before these movements become instinctive.

We began by asking whether a small robotic car could make this coordination easier to explore through an interactive experience.

Working with Hassan and Majid, I helped develop a gesture-controlled car that replaced the conventional remote with hand tracking and a physical foot pedal. By moving their hand in front of a camera, users could steer the car left or right, while the pedal provided control over its forward and backward movement.

The intention was to create a more physical connection between the user and the machine. Rather than pressing buttons on a controller, participants could use movements inspired by actual driving.

What began as an exploration of alternative control methods gradually developed into a racing experience, introducing an element of competition to the challenge of navigating a physical vehicle.

02

Design & Implementation

From Gesture to Movement

The robotic car, hand tracking, pedal mechanism, serial communication, and obstacle avoidance experiments.

We built the car using an Arduino Uno, four DC motors, and an L293D motor shield, which simplified the motor connections and left room for additional components.

Arduino Uno L293D Motor Shield 4× DC motors PoseNet · p5.js Foot pedal + potentiometer 2× Serial ports Ultrasonic + servo

The steering system was developed in p5.js using PoseNet to track the horizontal position of the user's right wrist through a webcam. We divided the tracking area into five regions, interpreting movement across them as steering instructions. Moving the hand left or right generated corresponding commands for the car.

For acceleration and braking, we developed a separate foot pedal mechanism using a potentiometer. Pressing the pedal changed its resistance reading, which Arduino transmitted to p5.js for interpretation.

Connecting everything presented another challenge. The car and pedal operated through separate Arduino boards, requiring two serial connections within the same p5.js application. We adapted the communication system to receive pedal inputs while sending movement commands to the car.

We also experimented with obstacle avoidance using an ultrasonic sensor mounted on a servo motor. This allowed the car to detect nearby obstacles and scan alternative directions, introducing an early form of environmental awareness alongside user control.

Interactive Racing Experience

Turning Movement into Competition

The racing circuit, ultrasonic lap timer, user interface, and leaderboard.

Controlling the car was only the beginning. We wanted to create an experience that encouraged people to test their coordination while making the interaction enjoyable.

We designed a racing circuit where participants could compete to complete a lap in the shortest possible time.

A separate Arduino system, connected to an ultrasonic sensor, monitored the start and finish line. When the car crossed the starting point, the system initiated a timer. Completing the circuit stopped the timer and recorded the lap duration.

We developed a leaderboard interface in p5.js to display recorded times. After finishing a lap, participants could enter their names and add their results, creating a running record of attempts.

The addition of timing and competition gave the project a different character. Steering accuracy and pedal control now had measurable consequences, turning an experimental control system into a shared interactive experience.

04

Challenges & Reflection

Engineering a Responsive Experience

Technical limitations, problem-solving, testing, and lessons from the project.

One of the greatest challenges was making the car respond reliably to gestures and pedal inputs.

Our initial hand-tracking implementation processed movement along both horizontal and vertical axes, placing unnecessary demands on the application. By focusing on horizontal wrist position, we reduced the amount of information being processed and improved responsiveness.

Communication between devices proved equally demanding. Our original plan involved wireless control, but difficulties with the Bluetooth hardware led us to use wired serial connections instead. Supporting two Arduino boards required changes to the p5.js serial implementation, while a USB extension cable provided enough freedom for the car to navigate the circuit.

These challenges taught me the importance of testing individual components before integrating them into a larger interactive system.

More importantly, the project demonstrated how software and physical mechanisms must work together to create a convincing interaction. A gesture is only useful when the machine responds in a way that feels understandable and consistent.

I became interested in how this approach could extend beyond recreational robotics, particularly toward systems that combine human input with environmental sensing to make physical interactions more responsive and accessible.
