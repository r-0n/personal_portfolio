Robotics · Speculative Interaction

The Crawler

A smartphone that refuses to remain passive

01

Concept & Inspiration

When the Phone Stops Waiting

We treat smartphones as tools that wait for us. They sit still until we decide to pick them up, unlock them, and look. The Crawler questions that assumption.

The project began with an interest in attention, dependency, and the quiet power devices already have over our posture, time, and presence in a room. What if that relationship became spatial? What if looking away — or looking too long — changed how the object behaved in physical space?

Rather than criticizing screen culture through a poster or interface, The Crawler turns dependency into movement. The phone is no longer only a surface for content. It becomes the head of a small robotic character that advances, retreats, and watches back.

02

Design Exploration

Giving Technology a Body

The central design move was simple: take a passive object people already hold and give it legs.

A six-legged chassis turned the smartphone into a character with weight, silhouette, and gait. The ultrasonic sensors read as eyes. Exposed wiring and servo joints kept the machine honest — a prototype that shows how it thinks, not a sealed appliance that hides its logic.

The phone mount places the screen where a face would be. When the display shows two glowing circles, the robot stops looking like hardware and starts looking like something that can meet your gaze.

03

Behavioral Design

Choreographing Attention

Behavior is the real interface. The Crawler is designed around three gestures: following, approaching, and withdrawing.

  • Follow — the robot orients toward presence, reading the room as something that can be pursued.
  • Approach — when attention leaves the glass, the machine closes distance, making reclaiming the world feel answered.
  • Withdraw — when screen-time dominates, it retreats, turning overuse into a visible social event.

These are not gags. They are a choreography of agency: the object stops waiting and starts negotiating space with the person who usually controls it.

04

Technical Development

From Intention to Movement

Under the character sits a control loop: sense, decide, actuate.

Ultrasonic sensing informs proximity. A microcontroller evaluates conditions and selects gait. Servo-driven legs translate those decisions into pursue and retreat locomotion across six limbs.

The technical work was always in service of the behavioral idea: movement had to feel intentional enough to be read as a response, not random noise.

05

Prototyping & Iteration

Building the Crawler

Building meant balancing stability, cable management, servo torque, and the awkward reality of carrying a glass rectangle on a walking chassis. Each test refined whether the wheel of attention — approach, retreat, pause — could be felt before it was explained.

06

Final Experiment

When the Object Moves First

The experiment is the encounter: a phone that no longer waits on the table, a body that closes or opens distance based on how you attend to it.

Documentation of that behavior will sit here as video arrives. For now, the stills hold the moment before motion — the character already looking, already capable of leaving.

07

Critical Reflection

Who Is Really in Control?

We like to believe we use devices. The Crawler makes that belief harder to hold without looking at it.

If a machine can answer your attention with movement, agency stops being a metaphor. Autonomy becomes a negotiation in the room: who approaches, who withdraws, whose gaze organizes the scene.

The project does not claim the robot is alive. It claims the relationship already was — and that giving the phone a body only made the psychology easier to see.

08

Future Directions

Beyond the Screen

Next steps push toward richer sensing, clearer gaits, and situations where physically expressive technology can live outside the studio — environments where a device’s body is part of how it communicates, not just how it is carried.

The Crawler is a first argument: the screen does not have to be the only surface of interaction. Sometimes the most honest interface is the one that walks toward you when you look away.
